Curb Rash Repair Cost: What You'll Actually Pay

Curb rash — the scuffed, gouged alloy where a wheel met a curb — costs $75 to $200 per wheel to refinish professionally. Machined or chrome finishes cost more, and a cracked wheel means replacement, not repair.

Light cosmetic scuff (DIY kit)

$20–$50

A DIY curb-rash kit with sandpaper, filler, and rattle-can paint can hide light scuffs on a painted alloy wheel. Good enough for a daily driver, not for a show car.

Professional alloy refinish

$75–$150

A wheel shop sands out the gouge, fills, repaints, and clear-coats the wheel off the car. Turnaround is usually 1–2 days per wheel.

Machined-finish wheel repair

$150–$300

Machined (diamond-cut) faces require a CNC lathe to restore, and only some shops have one. Over-machining thins the metal, so a wheel can only be re-cut a few times.

Chrome wheel refinish

$200–$400+

Chrome must be stripped and re-plated — a multi-step chemical process few shops do in-house. Badly pitted chrome often means replacement.

Cracked or bent wheel (replace)

$400–$900

A curb hit hard enough to crack or bend the rim is not a refinish job. A replacement OEM alloy typically runs $400–$900; forged or OEM-plus wheels cost more.

Does insurance cover curb rash?

Generally no, unless the damage is part of a larger covered incident (like a collision). Routine curb rash from parking is considered wear and tear, and most comprehensive policies exclude it. If a valet curbed your wheel during a handover, their liability is the better route — but again, only with proof it happened in their custody.

The valet curbed my wheel — what now?

Photograph the wheel before you leave the lot, then report it to the supervisor immediately while you are still on the property. A timestamped pre-handover photo of the clean wheel is what turns a denial (“it was already like that”) into a paid claim. Scanning the wheels as part of your pre-handover walk-around is the move.
