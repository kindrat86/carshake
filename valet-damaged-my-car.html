The first 10 minutes — before you leave the lot

The single biggest mistake people make is leaving the valet area before reporting the damage. Once you drive off, the valet company will almost always claim the damage happened afterward, and the dispute collapses into your word against theirs. Do this before you leave the property:

The first-10-minutes checklist
  1. Document the damage before anything else. Photograph and video every angle of the damage, the valet stand, the business name, and your ticket. Record a slow walkaround video. Don't move the car until it's documented.
  2. Find the supervisor or manager on duty. Don't argue with the attendant — go straight to the valet supervisor or the venue (hotel, restaurant, hospital) manager.
  3. Demand a written incident report — and keep a copy. Also collect: the attendant's name, the supervisor's contact details, your original valet ticket, and the exact drop-off and pick-up times.
  4. Request in writing that security footage be preserved. Valet-area cameras are often overwritten within days. A written preservation request creates a record that they were put on notice.
  5. Get any witnesses. If another guest or a parking attendant saw it, get their name and number. An independent witness breaks a he-said-she-said tie.
  6. Save your before-state proof. If you photographed or scanned the car when you arrived, that record is about to become the most valuable thing you own. (More on this below.)
If you already left the property: it's harder but not hopeless. Write down everything you remember right now while it's fresh, pull any photos or timestamps you have from arrival, and contact the venue and your insurer today. Most valet contracts require reporting within 24–48 hours — after that they deny claims outright.

Who is legally liable — and the theory that wins

When you hand your keys to a valet, two legal ideas are doing work for you at the same time:

1. Vicarious liability. Employers are generally responsible for the harm their employees cause while on the job. If a valet driver scraped your car while parking it, the valet company is on the hook — not just the individual driver. This matters because the individual attendant almost never has the assets to pay for a repair; the company does.

2. Bailment. The moment you hand over the keys you've created a bailment: the valet took temporary custody of your vehicle and is expected to return it in the condition it was received. That shifts the explanatory burden onto the valet — if the car came back damaged and was fine when you handed it over, the damage happened on their watch.

Here's the catch: in practice, valet operators almost never just accept this. Their play is to call the damage "pre-existing" or to wave the disclaimer on the ticket. Which is why the next two sections matter more than the legal theory.

Does the fine print on the valet ticket protect them?

Not as much as they'd like you to think. Liability waivers printed on valet tickets frequently fail to protect the parking service when the damage resulted from the valet's own negligence. Disclaimers of negligence are disfavored in contract law and are often unenforceable as a matter of public policy.

But — and this is the important part — the waiver doesn't have to be enforceable to work. It just has to scare you out of pursuing the claim. The valet company's bet is that you'll read "not responsible for damage," assume you have no recourse, and walk away. Their second bet, if you push, is that without proof of the before-state they can claim the damage was already there. The disclaimer is a fence; your evidence is what gets you over it.

Going through your own insurance & subrogation

If the valet company stonewalls you, your own auto policy is often the fastest path to getting the car fixed. Here's how it works:

StepWhat happens
1. File with your insurerCollision coverage pays for the repair, minus your deductible. (If you only have liability coverage, this route isn't available — go straight to the valet company and small claims.)
2. SubrogationYour insurer goes after the valet company's insurance to recover what it paid. You don't have to do the chasing — they do.
3. If they recoverMany insurers refund your deductible and remove the claim's rate impact once subrogation succeeds.

The trade-off is simple: you pay your deductible up front and get the car fixed fast, then potentially get the deductible back later. It's usually the right call when the valet company is denying everything and you need the car.

When small-claims court is the right move

If both the valet company and your insurer leave you hanging (or you don't carry collision coverage), small-claims court is a real option and is designed for exactly this kind of dispute. You don't need a lawyer, filing fees are low, and the filing limit varies by state — typically from a few thousand dollars up to $10,000 or more.

To win, you need to show three things: (1) the car was undamaged when you handed over the keys, (2) the damage existed when you got it back, and (3) the valet had custody in between. Notice that all three collapse into the same evidence: a timestamped record of the before-state. Which brings us to the most important section on this page.

Time limits: the two clocks working against you

Two separate deadlines will sink your claim if you miss them:

Clock 1 — the reporting window (days). Most valet contracts and venue policies require you to report damage within a short window — often 24 to 48 hours — or they deny the claim entirely. This is why reporting before you leave the lot matters.
Clock 2 — the statute of limitations (years). The legal deadline to file a property-damage lawsuit is set by your state and typically runs a few years, but it varies. If you might sue, don't sit on it. See your state's guide below for the specifics.

Frequently asked questions

What should I do immediately if a valet damaged my car?

Before leaving the property: photograph and video every angle of the damage, the valet stand, and the business; find the valet supervisor or venue manager and report it; ask for a written incident report and keep a copy; request in writing that security-camera footage be preserved; and collect the attendant's name, supervisor's contact details, your ticket, and the drop-off and pick-up times. Reporting after you leave the property dramatically weakens your claim.

Who is legally liable if a valet damages my car?

Generally the valet company is responsible for damage its employees cause during their job duties, under a principle called vicarious liability. When you hand over your keys you also create a bailment, meaning the valet took temporary custody and is expected to return the car in the condition received. In practice, valet operators often try to deny claims by calling damage pre-existing or by pointing to a ticket disclaimer — which is why proof of the before-state is decisive.

Does the fine print on the valet ticket protect the valet company?

Not automatically. Liability waivers printed on valet tickets frequently fail to protect the parking service when the damage resulted from the valet's own negligence. Disclaimers of negligence are disfavored and often unenforceable, but you still bear the burden of proving the valet caused the damage — which is why a timestamped before-state record matters so much.

Should I go through my own insurance if a valet damaged my car?

You can. If you carry collision coverage, your insurer can pay for the repair and then pursue the valet company's insurance to recover the cost through a process called subrogation. If subrogation succeeds, many insurers refund your deductible and remove the rate impact. Going through your own insurer is often faster than fighting the valet company directly, but you will initially pay your deductible.

Can I sue a valet company in small claims court?

Yes. If the valet company or its insurer refuses to pay, small claims court is a common and relatively low-cost way to recover the repair cost without a lawyer. The filing limit varies by state (typically a few thousand to $10,000+), and you will need to show the damage occurred during the valet's custody — again, a timestamped before-state record is the strongest evidence you can bring.

How long do I have to file a valet damage claim?

There are two clocks. Most valet companies and venue contracts require you to report damage within a short window (often 24 to 48 hours) or they deny the claim entirely — so report before you leave the property. The legal deadline to file a property-damage lawsuit is set by your state's statute of limitations, which is typically several years but varies.
